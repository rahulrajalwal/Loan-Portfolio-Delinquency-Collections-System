Loan Portfolio, Delinquency & Collections Intelligence System

Rahul Meena · IIT Gandhinagar · Portfolio Project · MySQL 8.0 and Python

A lending analytics system built on 58.4 million rows of real consumer-credit data. It answers a question every lender faces and most answer by instinct: when a collections team can only call a fraction of delinquent customers each day, which ones should they call first?

1. The problem statement

1.1 The business situation

A consumer lender holds a book of active loans and credit cards. At any moment, some customers are behind on payments. A collections team can contact only a limited number of them each day, so the order of that queue determines what actually gets recovered.

The instinctive ordering is by severity: call the customers who are furthest behind first. It feels obviously correct, because the worst accounts look like the biggest problem. Most collections queues are built exactly this way.

The question this project set out to test

Is "call the worst accounts first" actually the right ordering, and if not, what is the right one, and how much does the difference matter?

1.2 Who this system is for

This is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to assume the wrong thing.

  
It is nota screen where an agent looks up one customer and reads their balance and payment history. That is a customer-service tool.
It is nota report for the customer themselves.
It isa tool for the collections manager, who arrives on Monday with 7,211 delinquent accounts on the system, capacity for perhaps 300 calls that day, and a decision to make about where to point the team.

The output is therefore a ranked list, not a record. Who to call first, second, third.

1.3 The same problem, in a familiar setting

It is hospital triage

In an emergency room, patients are not treated in the order they arrived, and not by who has been waiting longest. They are treated by a combination of how serious the condition is and how treatable it is. Someone waiting six hours with a small cut goes after someone who just walked in with chest pain.

Sorting a collections queue by "most days overdue" is the same mistake as treating patients by "longest waiting". This project builds the triage rule instead: how much money is at stake, multiplied by how likely it is to actually come back. Both of those were measured from the data, not assumed.

1.4 The second question: does past behaviour predict future default?

A customer already holds a loan with the bank — a phone EMI, say, paid over the past year, some months on time and some late. That same customer now returns and asks for a new, larger loan.

The bank must decide whether to approve it. And the bank already holds this person's entire payment record on the existing loan.

Does how they paid the old loan tell you whether the new one will go bad?

It feels as though it must. The second half of this project tests whether it actually does, rather than assuming it.

The test is possible because the dataset contains 307,511 customers whose real outcome is already known — whether each one did in fact run into payment difficulty on the new loan. Every claim can therefore be checked against reality instead of argued for.

1.5 Why 58 million rows produce a 7,211-row answer

The scale needs explaining, because the input and the output look mismatched.

The 58 million rows are not the answer. They are how the rule is worked out. To establish that accounts 1 to 30 days late recover on their own roughly 55% of the time, while accounts past 90 days recover only about 5% of the time, you have to observe millions of accounts moving month by month over several years. That monthly history is what the large tables contain.

The heavy computation runs once, to learn how accounts behave. What is learned is then applied to today's 7,211 delinquent customers. Large input, small output — which is normal, and is why the architecture in section 4.4 separates the two.

1.6 Why these questions are hard

  • The evidence is buried in scale. Behavioural history lives in tables of 10 to 14 million rows. The answers are tables of a few dozen rows. Getting from one to the other is the entire task.
  • The obvious metric is misleading. "Days past due" is easy to sort by, but it measures something different from "money we might still recover".
  • Nobody sees the counterfactual. A badly ordered queue still looks busy. The loss is invisible unless someone measures it.

3. The approach

The work ran as a sequence of phases, each gated on evidence before the next began. One rule governed the whole project, and it overruled the plan three separate times.

Measure before deciding, and let the measurement overrule the assumption

What was assumedWhat measurement showed
The payment table holds one row per installmentWrong. One row is a payment event. 653,483 installments were settled by two or more payments; one by twelve.
Delinquency buckets should use the standard 30-day ladderOnly 542 accounts in the whole book ever peak in the 61-90 bucket. Too thin for stable rates, so two bucket schemes were built for two different purposes.
"Days late" should be measured at full settlementWrong. Lateness measured at the first payment separates the outcome far better (1.88x lift versus 1.33x).

Each of those was a reasonable prior belief that the data contradicted. Recording them, rather than silently correcting them, is part of what the project demonstrates.

4. The work, step by step

4.1 Understanding the structure — and finding a documented assumption to be wrong

Before any analysis, each table was tested for key uniqueness. Six of the seven behaved as expected. The seventh, and the largest, did not.

The documentation implied installments_payments held one row per installment. Testing the key showed 653,483 duplicate rows. Investigation showed they were not errors at all:

SK_ID_PREV 2270983, installment 30, due day -2022, amount due 9,000
  row 1:  paid day -2046,  amount 1,800   (24 days EARLY)
  row 2:  paid day -2019,  amount 7,200   ( 3 days LATE)
  total:  9,000  =  exactly the amount due

One installment, settled by two separate payments. Across the table, 99.94% of these multi-row cases summed exactly to the amount due. The real grain is one row per payment event.

Why this mattered more than it looks

Comparing AMT_PAYMENT to AMT_INSTALMENT row by row, the natural thing to write, flags 1,295,493 rows (9.52%) as underpayments. Aggregating first shows almost all of them were paid in full. That single mistake would have corrupted the entire repayment-behaviour analysis, and the resulting numbers would have looked completely plausible.

Fifteen checks were run. Two examples show the kind of issue found.

A sentinel value that inverts a conclusion

18.01% of applications carried DAYS_EMPLOYED = 365243, which decodes to employment starting roughly 1,000 years in the future. Cross-tabulation identified it precisely:

Analysis on the raw 13.8M-row monthly panel was measured at 275.7 seconds for a single roll-rate pass. Execution plans showed this could not be optimised away (see section 5.6), so the architecture changed instead: expensive computations run once into a pre-computed mart, and all analysis then runs against tables under 1M rows.

Step three: the comparison that settled it

The five-signal framework was tested against the simplest alternative available — just looking at the customer's age.

A young customer with a spotless payment record is riskier than an older customer with a bad one. Age alone separates the outcome better than the entire behavioural framework does.

What that result actually means — and what it does not

It would be easy to read the box above as "the signals do not work". That is the wrong conclusion. There are two separate effects here, and both are real.

Age bandZero signalsTwo or more signals
Under 3011.20%16.35%
30 to 399.22%12.51%
40 to 497.32%9.83%
50 to 595.83%8.72%
60 and over4.55%7.90%

Read the table across, then down.

  • Across each row: the signals work. In every single age band, risk rises by roughly 40 to 50% as signals accumulate. The framework is not broken.
  • Down the first column: 11.20% falls to 4.55%. The age effect is a 2.5x spread, while the signal effect within a band is about 1.5x.

So when the two extremes are compared — young and clean against old and troubled — age wins, and the younger customer comes out riskier. Not because the signals are meaningless, but because age moves the number further.

The same thing, in a setting everyone knows

Car insurance. A 20-year-old with a perfect driving record against a 60-year-old with two speeding tickets: the 20-year-old is the bigger risk, because age dominates. That does not make the tickets meaningless — among 20-year-olds, the one with tickets is still riskier than the one without. Both facts are true at once, and a good pricing model uses both.

The honest conclusion

Past payment behaviour does predict future default, but weakly, and less well than a single demographic attribute. The recommendation reflects that: use the signals on top of other information rather than instead of it — as a referral trigger, a reason to examine an application more carefully, and never as a standalone rule to decline someone.

The reason the framework is weak is coverage, not signal quality. The sharpest individual signals fire on roughly 1% of customers each. Most applicants trip no signal at all, so the framework has nothing to say about them.

One practical point that reverses the ranking

Age is a protected characteristic under lending regulation in most markets, and generally cannot be used to decide a credit application. So although age is statistically the stronger predictor here, it is largely unusable. Behavioural signals matter operationally precisely because they are information a lender is permitted to act on. A statistic being stronger does not make it the one you can use.

Why a weak result is reported rather than quietly dropped

The easy version of this analysis stops at the first table, reports "past lateness nearly doubles default risk", and moves on. Testing it against the dumbest available alternative — and finding that the alternative wins — is what distinguishes a finding that can be defended from one that merely sounds good. The same discipline produced two other negative results in this project: a widely assumed risk indicator ("repeated credit enquiries") turned out to carry no signal at all at 1.04x the base rate, and one of the performance optimisations attempted failed outright and is documented in section 5.6.

5.1 Finding the true grain CTE, aggregation

This is the query that overturned the documented assumption. It aggregates to the claimed key and asks whether the parts sum to the whole.

WITH per_installment AS (
    SELECT SK_ID_PREV, NUM_INSTALMENT_VERSION, NUM_INSTALMENT_NUMBER,
           COUNT(*)            AS n_payments,
           MAX(AMT_INSTALMENT) AS amt_due,
           SUM(AMT_PAYMENT)    AS amt_paid
    FROM installments_payments
    GROUP BY SK_ID_PREV, NUM_INSTALMENT_VERSION, NUM_INSTALMENT_NUMBER
)
SELECT COUNT(*)                                               AS installments_total,
       SUM(n_payments > 1)                                    AS installments_split,
       MAX(n_payments)                                        AS max_payments_one_installment,
       SUM(n_payments > 1 AND ABS(amt_paid - amt_due) < 0.01) AS split_and_fully_settled
FROM per_installment;

Result: 12,951,918 installments, 640,905 settled by multiple payments, a maximum of 12 payments for a single installment, and 99.94% summing exactly to the amount due. The SUM(condition) form works because MySQL evaluates a boolean to 1 or 0, so a count and a filter happen in one pass.

5.2 The roll-rate matrix LAG, window partitioning, gap guard

The core delinquency question: what is the probability an account in one state this month is in another state next month? This requires comparing consecutive rows within each account.

WITH seq AS (
    SELECT PRODUCT, SK_ID_PREV, MONTHS_BALANCE, BUCKET_ANALYTICAL,
           LAG(BUCKET_ANALYTICAL) OVER (PARTITION BY SK_ID_PREV, PRODUCT
                                        ORDER BY MONTHS_BALANCE) AS from_bucket,
           LAG(MONTHS_BALANCE)    OVER (PARTITION BY SK_ID_PREV, PRODUCT
                                        ORDER BY MONTHS_BALANCE) AS prev_mb
    FROM stg_account_month
)
SELECT PRODUCT, from_bucket, BUCKET_ANALYTICAL AS to_bucket, COUNT(*) AS transitions
FROM seq
WHERE from_bucket IS NOT NULL
  AND MONTHS_BALANCE = prev_mb + 1          -- consecutive months only
GROUP BY PRODUCT, from_bucket, BUCKET_ANALYTICAL;

The line that matters most

AND MONTHS_BALANCE = prev_mb + 1. Consecutive rows are not necessarily consecutive months — the panel has gaps. Without this guard, a jump from month -8 to month -3 would be recorded as a single month's transition, inventing movements that never happened. The number of pairs rejected for this reason was measured and reported (0.02%) rather than assumed to be zero.

5.3 Comparing two orderings of the same rows dual ROW_NUMBER, CROSS JOIN

The project's central result. The same 7,211 accounts are ranked two ways, and the question asked is how much recoverable value each ordering reaches for a given amount of effort.

WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT cure_weighted_exposure AS v,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY cure_weighted_exposure DESC) AS rn_value,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY DPD_NOW DESC)                AS rn_dpd,
           COUNT(*)                    OVER () AS n,
           SUM(cure_weighted_exposure) OVER () AS total_value
    FROM mart_collections_queue
)
SELECT pct_worked,
       ROUND(100.0*SUM(CASE WHEN rn_value <= n*pct_worked/100 THEN v ELSE 0 END)
             /MAX(total_value), 2) AS pct_value_reached_by_VALUE,
       ROUND(100.0*SUM(CASE WHEN rn_dpd   <= n*pct_worked/100 THEN v ELSE 0 END)
             /MAX(total_value), 2) AS pct_value_reached_by_DPD
FROM ranked
CROSS JOIN (SELECT 5 AS pct_worked UNION SELECT 10 UNION SELECT 20
            UNION SELECT 30 UNION SELECT 50) p
GROUP BY pct_worked ORDER BY pct_worked;

Two ROW_NUMBER windows over the same rows give two orderings side by side. The CROSS JOIN against a small inline list produces every capacity level in one query instead of five. The empty OVER () frames compute whole-set totals without a second pass.

5.4 Evaluating signals against an observed outcome CTE, CROSS JOIN, conditional aggregation

Rather than asserting that certain behaviours indicate risk, each candidate signal was measured against the observed 8.0729% base rate, and the precision-versus-coverage trade-off computed at every threshold.

WITH scored AS (
    SELECT TARGET,
           (N_LATE_FIRST_30PLUS > 0) + (N_SHORTFALL > 0) + (N_BUREAU_OVERDUE > 0)
         + (N_DETERIORATING_ACCOUNTS > 0) + (MONTHS_DELINQUENT >= 3) AS sc
    FROM mart_customer WHERE SOURCE = 'train'
), tot AS (SELECT COUNT(*) n, SUM(TARGET) d FROM scored)
SELECT th AS threshold,
       SUM(sc >= th)                                                  AS flagged,
       ROUND(100.0*SUM(CASE WHEN sc >= th THEN TARGET ELSE 0 END)
             / NULLIF(SUM(sc >= th),0), 3)                            AS precision_pct,
       ROUND(100.0*SUM(CASE WHEN sc >= th THEN TARGET ELSE 0 END)
             / MAX(tot.d), 3)                                         AS recall_pct
FROM scored CROSS JOIN tot
CROSS JOIN (SELECT 1 AS th UNION SELECT 2 UNION SELECT 3 UNION SELECT 4 UNION SELECT 5) thr
GROUP BY th ORDER BY th;

Adding booleans gives a signal count without a CASE ladder. The result was reported honestly: the framework reaches only a 1.80x lift at usable volume, and scores of 1 and 2 turned out statistically indistinguishable — a genuine weakness that was published rather than hidden.

5.5 Demonstrating the fan-out error join cardinality

The same business question asked two ways, to quantify what a careless join costs.

-- CORRECT: the customer table is already at customer grain
SELECT NAME_INCOME_TYPE, SUM(AMT_CREDIT) FROM mart_customer GROUP BY NAME_INCOME_TYPE;

-- WRONG: joining to payment events repeats AMT_CREDIT once per payment
SELECT c.NAME_INCOME_TYPE, SUM(c.AMT_CREDIT)
FROM mart_customer c JOIN stg_installment i ON i.SK_ID_CURR = c.SK_ID_CURR

MethodResultRows aggregatedRuntime
Correct209.40 bn356,2550.69 s

The wrong answer is 37.5 times too large and 140 times slower. The detail that proves the point: rows inflated 36.4x but the value inflated 37.5x. If the multiplier were constant it could be divided out; because it varies per customer, the join silently re-weights the whole portfolio toward customers with more payment history.

5.6 An optimisation that failed, and why that is worth reporting

The roll-rate query took 275.7 seconds. The hypothesis was that it partitioned by SK_ID_PREV while the primary key was (SK_ID_PREV, PRODUCT, MONTHS_BALANCE), forcing an unnecessary sort. Aligning them should have let the engine read rows already in order.

-> Window aggregate with buffering: lag(...) OVER (PARTITION BY SK_ID_PREV ORDER BY MONTHS_BALANCE)
    -> Sort: SK_ID_PREV, MONTHS_BALANCE   (cost=1.45e+6 rows=13.2e+6)
        -> Index scan using ix_am_bucket

The hypothesis was refuted: 253.4 s against 271.9 s, and both plans performed a full sort of 13.2 million rows regardless. MySQL 8 always materialises and sorts for a buffered window function and will not use clustered-index order to avoid it. This is an engine limitation, not a query-writing mistake, and no index fixes it.

The response was to stop optimising the query and change the architecture: compute the matrix once into a 40-row table. 275.7 s became 0.09 s.

6.2 Profiling 58 million rows within 7.3 GB of memory

The initial data profiling ran on the raw CSVs before anything reached MySQL, on a machine with 7.3 GB of RAM against 2.68 GB of files. This required chunked, streaming accumulation rather than loading tables whole:

for ch in pd.read_csv(path, chunksize=1_500_000, low_memory=False):
    for c in ch.columns:
        nulls[c] += int(ch[c].isna().sum())
        if pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(ch[c]):
            v = ch[c].dropna()
            mn[c] = v.min() if c not in mn else min(mn[c], v.min())
            mx[c] = v.max() if c not in mx else max(mx[c], v.max())
    del ch; gc.collect()

The same approach checked key uniqueness on a 27.3M-row table by loading only the two key columns, keeping memory bounded regardless of table width.

6.3 Code generation

The load scripts were generated rather than hand-written. With 219 columns across eight files, every nullable column needed NULLIF(@var, '') handling and every column list had to match its CSV header exactly. A Python script parsed the schema for nullability, read each CSV header, and emitted the LOAD DATA statements — removing an entire class of transcription error that would have been invisible until the numbers came out wrong.

Q3. Prove the new ordering is better than the obvious one

WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT cure_weighted_exposure AS v,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY cure_weighted_exposure DESC) AS rn_value,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY DPD_NOW DESC)                AS rn_dpd,
           COUNT(*)                    OVER () AS n,
           SUM(cure_weighted_exposure) OVER () AS total_value
    FROM mart_collections_queue
)
SELECT pct_worked AS pct_of_capacity,
       ROUND(100.0*SUM(CASE WHEN rn_value <= n*pct_worked/100 THEN v ELSE 0 END)
             /MAX(total_value), 2) AS reached_sorting_by_VALUE,
       ROUND(100.0*SUM(CASE WHEN rn_dpd   <= n*pct_worked/100 THEN v ELSE 0 END)
             /MAX(total_value), 2) AS reached_sorting_by_DPD
FROM ranked
CROSS JOIN (SELECT 5 AS pct_worked UNION SELECT 10 UNION SELECT 20
            UNION SELECT 30 UNION SELECT 50) p
GROUP BY pct_worked ORDER BY pct_worked;

Q6. Do the five signals combined work?

SELECT signal_count,
       COUNT(*)                        AS customers,
       ROUND(100.0*AVG(TARGET), 2)     AS went_bad_pct,
       ROUND(AVG(TARGET)/0.080729, 2)  AS vs_base_rate
FROM (
    SELECT TARGET,
           (COALESCE(N_LATE_FIRST_30PLUS,0) > 0)        -- 1 ever 30+ days late
         + (COALESCE(N_SHORTFALL,0) > 0)                -- 2 paid short
         + (COALESCE(N_BUREAU_OVERDUE,0) > 0)           -- 3 overdue elsewhere
         + (COALESCE(N_DETERIORATING_ACCOUNTS,0) > 0)   -- 4 getting worse
         + (COALESCE(MONTHS_DELINQUENT,0) >= 3)         -- 5 chronically behind
           AS signal_count
    FROM mart_customer WHERE SOURCE='train'
) t
GROUP BY signal_count ORDER BY signal_count;

Returns: 0 signals = 7.76%, 1 = 10.02%, 2 = 10.03%, 3 = 13.97%, 4 = 20.00%. Rising, but note scores 1 and 2 are effectively identical. Adding booleans works because MySQL evaluates each comparison to 1 or 0.

Q7. The decisive test — do the signals beat simply looking at age?

SELECT CASE WHEN AGE_YEARS < 30 THEN '1: under 30' WHEN AGE_YEARS < 40 THEN '2: 30-39'
            WHEN AGE_YEARS < 50 THEN '3: 40-49'    WHEN AGE_YEARS < 60 THEN '4: 50-59'
            ELSE '5: 60+' END                                    AS age_band,
       COUNT(*)                                                  AS customers,
       ROUND(100.0*AVG(CASE WHEN sc  = 0 THEN TARGET END), 2)    AS zero_signals_pct,
       ROUND(100.0*AVG(CASE WHEN sc >= 2 THEN TARGET END), 2)    AS two_plus_signals_pct
FROM (

         + (COALESCE(MONTHS_DELINQUENT,0) >= 3) AS sc
    FROM mart_customer WHERE SOURCE='train'
) t
GROUP BY age_band ORDER BY age_band;
